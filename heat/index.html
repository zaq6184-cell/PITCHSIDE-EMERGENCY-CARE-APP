HEAT ILLNESS

Exertional heat stroke — cool first, transport second

The heat emergency that kills on the pitch — and is survivable when cold-water cooling starts at once.

  • Suspect it in any player who collapses or behaves oddly in the heat.
  • Measure a rectal temperature: above 40 °C with a change in thinking is heat stroke. Cannot measure it? Treat it as heat stroke anyway.
  • Cool in stirred cold water until about 39 °C — then transport. Call the ambulance at once, but cooling comes first.

HEAT RISK TODAY

WBGT combines air temperature, humidity, sun and wind. FIFA measures it at the pitch 90 and 60 minutes before kick-off; check it again at half-time, then drag the reading.

HIGH RISK

FIFPRO: cooling breaks above WBGT 26 °C

  • Cooling breaks around the 30th minute of each half.
  • Ice towels, cold drinks and shade ready for every break.
  • Cold-water immersion tub set up and filled before kick-off.
  • Watch the players most at risk: just back from illness, not used to the heat, the hardest workers.

World Cup 2026: every match stopped for a 3-minute hydration break 22 minutes into each half, whatever the temperature. Your competition's own heat rules always decide.

SPOT EXERTIONAL HEAT STROKE

  • Thinking changesConfused, irritable, aggressive, odd behaviour, not answering properly.
  • Staggering or collapseUnsteady, falls, or collapses — often late in a hot match or session.
  • SeizureA fit or a fall in consciousness.
  • Rectal temperature above 40 °CThe only reliable reading after exercise. Oral, ear, forehead and armpit readings miss heat stroke.
  • Fast pulse, low blood pressureBreathing fast; the skin may be sweaty and even cool — that does not rule it out.

Heat exhaustion: weak, dizzy, with a headache or nausea, perhaps collapsed, but thinking clearly and with a rectal temperature below 40 °C — shade, lie flat with the legs raised, loosen the kit, and fluids by mouth if not nauseous. Any change in thinking: check the glucose and treat it as heat stroke.

Check the blood glucose too: a low sugar often comes with heat stroke and needs its own treatment. Confused with a rectal temperature below 40 °C? Think of low sugar, or of low sodium from drinking too much water (hyponatraemia): hospital.

COOL FIRST, TRANSPORT SECOND

The aim is below 39 °C within 30 minutes of the collapse; the time spent above 40.5 °C is what damages the brain, kidneys and liver. Pick a method and where cooling starts, then run the clock.

Cold-water immersion (tub, stirred): about 0.20 °C a minute (studies: 0.15–0.35). Fast enough — experts ask for at least 0.155 °C a minute. Into the tub about 3 minutes after the collapse.

SET UP COLD-WATER IMMERSION

  1. 1On hot days, before kick-off: a tub (or a tarp), water and plenty of ice at the pitch, a rectal thermometer, towels and a sheet.
  2. 2Call the ambulance — then cool. Take off the kit quickly and lower the player into the water up to the neck, head out, supported under the armpits with a sheet or towel.
  3. 3Ice water, about 1–17 °C. Keep stirring it and keep adding ice.
  4. 4Rectal temperature every 5 minutes; a probe left in place is best. Check the blood glucose as well.
  5. 5Out of the water at about 39 °C, so the player does not overcool. Dry, keep checking, then transfer to hospital.
  6. 6No tub? Ice water in a tarp held around the player, or ice-water towels over the whole body, swapped every few minutes.

DO NOT

  • Wait for the ambulance before cooling.
  • Trust an oral, ear, forehead or armpit temperature.
  • Give paracetamol or ibuprofen — they do not lower the temperature in heat stroke and can do harm.
  • Rely on ice packs at the neck, armpits and groin on their own — far too slow.
  • Let the player go back on the same day.
