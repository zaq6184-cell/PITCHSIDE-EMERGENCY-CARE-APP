Medical emergencies

Not every collapse is cardiac or trauma. These cards follow chapters 4 and 5 of the FIFA Emergency Care Manual, with the adult doses it gives. Give drugs only within your training, follow your local protocols, and afterwards complete any anti-doping follow-up.

First, every time: a collapsed player who is not breathing normally is in cardiac arrest. Start CPR and get the AED on.

Anaphylaxis

A severe allergic reaction: a sting, a food, a medicine or exercise

AI-generated illustration

RECOGNISE

  • Sudden trouble with the airway (swollen lips, tongue or throat, a hoarse voice, noisy breathing), the breathing (wheeze, breathlessness) or the circulation (pale, clammy, faint, collapse).
  • Usually with hives, flushing or swelling of the skin, but these are missing in up to 1 in 5 cases.
  • Often within minutes of a sting, a food, a medicine or exercise.

WHAT TO DO

  1. Call the ambulance at once and say “anaphylaxis”.
  2. Adrenaline into the outer thigh straight away, through clothing if need be: the player's own auto-injector, or the bag's.
  3. Faint or collapsed: lie the player flat with the legs raised. Trouble with the airway or breathing: let them find their most comfortable position, often sitting up, and do not force them to lie down. Do not let them stand or walk.
  4. Remove the trigger if you can: scrape out a sting.
  5. Repeat the adrenaline after 5 minutes if there is no improvement. If they stop breathing normally, start CPR.

IF TRAINED

  • Adrenaline 1 mg/mL (1:1000): 0.5 mL (500 micrograms) IM into the anterolateral thigh, drawn up from a 1 mL ampoule if there is no auto-injector. An EpiPen gives 300 micrograms, so a repeat dose is more often needed.
  • Not the 1:10 000 prefilled syringe in the bag: that is for cardiac arrest. No IV adrenaline before hospital unless you are experienced and the player is monitored.
  • Oxygen for a collapse or a low SpO₂, to keep it at 94–98%. Wheeze: salbutamol by inhaler and spacer, or by oxygen-driven nebuliser, as often as needed.
  • Circulation failing: IV access after the adrenaline, then 500–1000 mL of crystalloid over 5–10 minutes. No better after two doses of adrenaline: refractory anaphylaxis, which needs advanced care urgently.
  • Chlorpheniramine and hydrocortisone are not emergency treatment: if given at all, only after the primary assessment and the IM adrenaline.

Call the ambulance: Always. Even after a good response the player goes to hospital: the reaction can come back hours later.

In football: Bee and wasp stings on the pitch, nuts in team food, anti-inflammatory painkillers (NSAIDs) taken before a match, and exercise itself. A player with a known severe allergy keeps two auto-injectors together and with them at all times, not one in the car and one in the wash bag.

Source: FIFA Emergency Care Manual, chapter 4: choking and anaphylaxis; Resuscitation Council UK, Emergency treatment of anaphylaxis (2021).

Asthma attack

Wheeze and breathlessness the usual inhaler does not settle

AI-generated illustration

RECOGNISE

  • Breathless, wheezy, coughing, a tight chest: often during or just after hard exercise, or in cold air or pollen.
  • Severe: peak flow 33–50% of best, cannot finish a sentence, breathing 25 or more a minute, pulse 110 or more.
  • Life-threatening: peak flow below 33% of best, SpO₂ below 92%, a quiet (silent) chest, blue lips, exhaustion or confusion.

WHAT TO DO

  1. Sit the player up and keep them calm.
  2. Reliever inhaler (salbutamol) through a spacer, one puff at a time with normal breaths: another puff every 60 seconds, up to 10 puffs.
  3. No better after 10 puffs, or any severe sign: call the ambulance, and repeat the 10 puffs every 20 minutes while you wait.
  4. If they collapse or stop breathing normally, start CPR.

IF TRAINED

  • Oxygen to keep SpO₂ at 94–98%.
  • Nebulised salbutamol 5 mg, driven by oxygen at 6 L/min; add ipratropium 0.5 mg if severe or not responding. Repeat the salbutamol every 20 minutes as needed.
  • Prednisolone 40–50 mg by mouth, or hydrocortisone 100 mg IV if they cannot swallow.
  • Wheeze with swelling, hives or a falling blood pressure after a trigger: treat it as anaphylaxis, with IM adrenaline.
  • The anti-doping rules still apply: nebulised salbutamol and oral or IV steroids may need a retroactive therapeutic use exemption (TUE).

Call the ambulance: Any severe or life-threatening sign, no relief from the inhaler, or any doubt. The player travels with the treatment still running.

In football: Exercise-induced asthma is common in footballers and is often worst 5–10 minutes after exercise stops. Check that each player who needs one has a reliever and spacer on the bench. No spacer: make one from a 500 mL plastic bottle with a hole in its base for the inhaler.

Source: FIFA Emergency Care Manual, chapter 5: asthma and exercise-induced bronchospasm, using the BTS/SIGN British guideline on the management of asthma (2019).

Low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia)

A player with diabetes, or a long session without food

AI-generated illustration

RECOGNISE

  • Shaky, sweaty, pale, hungry, irritable or confused. Slurred speech or odd behaviour can look like exhaustion, heat illness, drunkenness or a head injury.
  • Blood glucose below 4 mmol/L (70 mg/dL).
  • It can lead to a seizure or unconsciousness.

WHAT TO DO

  1. The player leaves the pitch at once to have the blood glucose checked. No meter to hand: treat anyway.
  2. Awake and able to swallow: at least 15–20 g of fast-acting glucose, such as dextrose tablets or gel, or 150–200 mL of fruit juice. Most isotonic sports drinks do not contain enough.
  3. Recheck after 10–15 minutes, and repeat until the glucose is above 4 mmol/L.
  4. Once better, give a longer-acting carbohydrate: a sandwich, a banana or the next meal.
  5. Drowsy or unconscious: nothing by mouth. Airway first: turn them onto their side and keep the airway open, then call the ambulance.

IF TRAINED

  • Glucagon 1 mg IM (the prefilled syringe in the FIFA bag), or the player's own nasal glucagon, once the airway is safe.
  • IV glucose: 10% in 100–200 mL amounts, repeated until the player is alert or the glucose is above 4 mmol/L. Use 20% or 50% only if there is no 10%: less volume, but thicker and harder on the veins.
  • Home, with advice, only when fully awake, able to swallow and give their history, and with someone to look after them. Otherwise, hospital.

Call the ambulance: Unconscious, fitting, no better after three treatments, or any doubt about the cause.

In football: Players on insulin carry fast sugar and their meter. A low can come hours after a match or training, and can happen without diabetes after intense exercise or in the heat. Check the glucose of any confused or collapsed player.

Source: FIFA Emergency Care Manual, chapter 5: hypoglycaemia; Joint British Diabetes Societies, The hospital management of hypoglycaemia in adults with diabetes mellitus.

Seizure (fit)

Stiffening and jerking, with loss of awareness

AI-generated illustration

RECOGNISE

  • Stiffening, then rhythmic jerking. The player may cry out, go blue, bite their tongue or wet themselves.
  • Afterwards drowsy and confused for a while.
  • A player who collapses without contact and then jerks is in cardiac arrest until proven otherwise: check the breathing at once. Gasping is not normal breathing.

WHAT TO DO

  1. Note the time it started.
  2. Protect, do not restrain: clear the space, cushion the head and loosen anything tight. Nothing in the mouth. Turn them onto their side if you can.
  3. When it stops, check the airway and breathing. Not breathing normally: start CPR and get the AED on.
  4. Breathing normally: recovery position (log roll with the head held if the neck may be injured).
  5. Treat every seizure as low blood sugar until a reading shows otherwise: check the glucose and treat a low as on its card. Stay with the player until fully awake.

IF TRAINED

  • Fitting for 5 minutes or more, or again without waking, once a low sugar is ruled out or treated: midazolam 10 mg buccal, intranasal or IM, the drug of choice in football.
  • With IV access: lorazepam 4 mg IV (0.1 mg/kg), or if there is none, diazepam 5–10 mg IV, as in the FIFA bag. Still fitting 5 minutes after a dose: consider repeating it.
  • Have airway adjuncts, a bag-valve mask and oxygen ready whenever you give a benzodiazepine: it can slow the breathing.

Call the ambulance: A first seizure, one lasting 5 minutes or more, a repeat, an injury, a cause such as a head impact or low sugar, trouble breathing or not waking normally afterwards, or any anticonvulsant given. They travel on their side.

In football: A seizure straight after a head impact (an impact seizure) is a red flag in FIFA's concussion protocol: the player comes off, does not return that day, and goes to hospital for a head scan the same day.

Source: FIFA Emergency Care Manual, chapter 5: seizures; FIFA medical concussion protocol.

Choking

Something stuck in the airway: food, gum, a mouthguard

AI-generated illustration

RECOGNISE

  • Mild: can speak, cough and breathe. Encourage coughing.
  • Severe: cannot speak, cough or breathe; may clutch the throat and turn blue.
  • Choking and anaphylaxis can both block the airway: what a witness saw (eating, a sting, a medicine) tells them apart.

WHAT TO DO

  1. Severe, still conscious: stand behind the player, lean them forward and give up to 5 back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
  2. Not cleared: from behind, put your arms around them, a fist between the navel and the bottom of the breastbone with your other hand over it, and give up to 5 abdominal thrusts, pulling sharply in and up.
  3. Stop and reassess as soon as anything changes; otherwise keep alternating 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts.
  4. Unresponsive: lower them to the ground, call the ambulance and start CPR, as in cardiac arrest. Look in the mouth before each breath and remove anything you can see; no blind finger sweeps.

IF TRAINED

  • Suction, and the Magill forceps from the FIFA bag, to lift out an object you can see; then open the airway and use adjuncts as in cardiac arrest.
  • Anyone who needed abdominal thrusts is checked by a doctor afterwards for internal injury.

Call the ambulance: Severe choking, any unresponsiveness, and whenever abdominal thrusts were needed.

In football: A player cannot swallow their tongue. In a collapsed player, open the airway (a jaw thrust if the neck may be injured) instead of reaching into the mouth, and lift out a loose mouthguard you can see.

Source: FIFA Emergency Care Manual, chapter 4: choking and anaphylaxis, with the Resuscitation Council UK adult choking algorithm (2021).
